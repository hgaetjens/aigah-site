Farm automation failures: an evidence bank and a review of reporting duties

Sinta Fragkou, mentored by Henrike Gätjens

22 incidents documented

Summary by Sinta Fragkou

What this project is

Farms across Europe now depend on automated systems to keep animals alive. Some of these systems are the ventilation, oxygen supply, water treatment, lighting, milking and feeding. When these systems fail, thousands of animals can die within hours. This project documents those failures and asks whether the law makes anyone report them.

Farm automation is already killing animals at an appalling scale and reporting duties do not capture these cases in detail. This is especially important since farms are moving from simple automation towards AI-run monitoring and control. Today's failures are the early warning for AI-run farms and a chance to fix reporting before that shift happens.

The two resources

Evidence bank
22 documented incidents where farm automation or connected systems failed and animals died. Each entry records the country, species, what failed, how strong the evidence is, estimated costs and how the harm is categorised.
Reporting duties
A country-by-country table of the legal duties to report farmed animal deaths, including those from automation failures, for salmon, trout, pigs, hens and cows. This includes England, Scotland, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, France, Switzerland, Belgium, Denmark and Germany.

Headline numbers

  • 22 incidents across 8 countries, from 2018 to early 2026.
  • ~5 million farmed fish dead, all at land-based salmon hatcheries in Scotland.
  • ~117,000 land animals dead: about 110,000 poultry and 7,000 pigs
  • ~4 million of those fish died at one site complex, Bakkafrost's Applecross hatcheries, across five events between 2022 and 2026
  • Only 3 incidents led to a criminal conviction, one of which ended with no penalty.

Note from AIGAH

AIGAH checked the EU rules and some of the national rules in Sinta's country table against the legal texts. Under the EU's Animal Health Law (Regulation (EU) 2016/429), farm operators must notify a veterinarian of abnormal mortalities “with an undetermined cause”. We found no EU-wide duty to notify anyone when the cause of the deaths is known, for example when a ventilation system fails.

As far as the sources show, no incident in the evidence bank involves machine learning. In 20 of the 22 incidents the system that failed was mechanical, electrical or a computerised control, such as a ventilation controller or an alarm, and in two incidents the type of system is unknown. These layers of automation are the basis on which AI systems are being added right now.

Eleven of the 22 incidents come from two public registers, one in Scotland and one in France (the mortality reports that fish farms send voluntarily to Scotland's Fish Health Inspectorate, and the French ARIA database of accidents). In the ten jurisdictions in Sinta's table we found no other official register of this kind that the public can read. The other eleven incidents happened in England, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland, Denmark and Germany, and they became known through media reports, court rulings or other official records. Failures that were never recorded or reported are missing from the evidence bank, because the information did not leave the farm. The evidence bank therefore cannot show where failures happen most often.
